COURSE OVERVIEW
Cybersecurity Fundamentals introduces learners to the safe and responsible protection of devices, accounts,
networks and information. The course focuses on defensive security, risk awareness, secure habits, incident
reporting, data protection and professional cybersecurity pathways.

Practicals use safe demonstrations, fictional examples, account-security checks and approved training
scenarios. Learners are not asked to attack real systems, bypass security controls or obtain other people's
credentials.

 Explain confidentiality, integrity and availability.
 Identify assets, threats, vulnerabilities, risks and security controls.
 Protect accounts with strong unique passwords and multi-factor authentication.
 Recognise phishing and social-engineering warning signs.
 Understand common malware categories and defensive measures.
 Apply safe update, device and network practices.
 Protect personal and organisational information.
 Understand backups and recovery.
 Apply least privilege and role-based access concepts.
 Respond appropriately to suspected incidents.
 Understand privacy, ethics and responsible cybersecurity practice.
 Identify professional cybersecurity career pathways.

LESSON 1: CYBERSECURITY FOUNDATIONS
Cybersecurity protects digital systems and information from unauthorized access, disruption, alteration and
loss.

 Figure 1. The confidentiality, integrity and availability security model.

The CIA Triad
 Confidentiality - information is available only to authorised people.
 Integrity - information remains accurate and is changed only in authorised ways.
 Availability - systems and information are accessible when authorised users need them.

People, Process and Technology
Security depends on responsible users, clear procedures and appropriate technical controls working together.

Everyday Cybersecurity
Examples include locking a device, using unique passwords, checking suspicious messages, updating
software and backing up important work.

Safe Guided Practical
1. Identify five digital assets you use for school or work.

2. For each asset, state why confidentiality, integrity or availability matters.

3. Create a personal safe-computing checklist without including passwords.

Review Questions
1. What is cybersecurity?

2. Explain confidentiality.

 

3. Explain integrity.

4. Explain availability.

Assignment
Prepare a one-page summary of the CIA triad.

 

LESSON 2: ASSETS, THREATS, VULNERABILITIES AND RISK
Risk becomes easier to manage when we distinguish what we value, what can cause harm, where
weaknesses exist and which controls reduce exposure.

 Figure 2. Assets, threats, vulnerabilities and controls.

Assets
Assets include devices, accounts, files, services, reputation and business information.

Threats
Threats include scams, malware, theft, accidental loss, service failure and unauthorized access.

Vulnerabilities
A vulnerability is a weakness, such as outdated software, an unlocked device or excessive permissions.

Controls
Controls reduce risk. Examples include updates, MFA, backups, access restrictions and awareness training.

Risk Thinking
A simple risk assessment considers likelihood and impact. High-impact risks deserve stronger attention even
when they are not frequent.

Safe Guided Practical
1. Choose a fictional small training centre.

2. List five assets.

3. For each asset, identify one possible threat and one defensive control.

4. Rank the risks as Low, Medium or High using your reasoned judgement.
 

Review Questions
1. What is an asset?

2. Differentiate threat and vulnerability.

3. What is a security control?

4. What two factors are commonly considered in simple risk ranking?

Assignment
Create a risk register for five fictional assets.

 

LESSON 3: PASSWORDS, PASSPHRASES AND MFA
Account security begins with strong authentication and careful credential handling.

 Figure 3. Passwords and multi-factor authentication.

Strong Password Practice
 Use long, unique passwords or passphrases.
 Do not reuse important passwords across services.
 Do not share passwords with classmates or colleagues.
 Avoid predictable personal information.

Password Managers
A reputable password manager can help generate and store unique passwords. Protect the manager itself
with strong authentication.

Multi-Factor Authentication
MFA requires an additional approved factor beyond the password, reducing the impact of a stolen password.

Recovery Information
Keep recovery methods current and secure. Do not expose recovery codes in screenshots or class
submissions.

Safe Guided Practical
1. Review the security settings of an account you are authorised to use.

2. Check whether MFA is available.

3. Create three fictional example passphrases; do not use real passwords.

4. Write a recovery checklist that excludes secret codes.
 

Review Questions
1. Why should passwords be unique?

2. What is MFA?

3. Why can password reuse be dangerous?

4. What information should never be submitted in a class exercise?

Assignment
Create an account-security policy for a fictional student portal.

 

LESSON 4: PHISHING AND SOCIAL ENGINEERING AWARENESS
Social engineering attempts to influence people into revealing information, sending money or taking unsafe
actions.

 Figure 4. Warning signs in a fictional phishing message.

Common Warning Signs
 Unexpected urgency or threats
 Requests for passwords or one-time codes
 Unusual payment requests
 Mismatched sender identity
 Suspicious links or attachments
 Offers that seem unrealistic

Safe Verification
Do not rely only on the message itself. Verify important requests using an official website, known phone
number or trusted person.

Phishing Beyond Email
Scams can arrive through SMS, messaging apps, social media, voice calls and fake websites.

Reporting
Use the organisation's reporting process. Reporting quickly can protect other users.

Safe Guided Practical
1. Review three fictional messages supplied by the instructor.

Review Questions

2. Name five warning signs.

3. Why should important requests be verified separately?

4. Where can phishing messages appear?

Assignment

LESSON 5: MALWARE AND ENDPOINT PROTECTION
Malware is software designed to cause harm, disrupt systems, steal information or enable unauthorized
activity.

 Figure 5. Malware categories and defensive measures.

Common Categories
 Virus
 Worm
 Trojan
 Ransomware
 Spyware

Endpoint Protection
Defensive measures include supported operating systems, timely updates, trusted security software, limited
permissions and safe downloading habits.

Backups
Backups help recovery when files are lost, damaged or made unavailable.

Safe Learning Rule
Do not download, create or run malware for this course. Use diagrams and approved simulations only.

LESSON 6: UPDATES, PATCHING AND SECURE CONFIGURATION
Software updates can correct security weaknesses, fix defects and improve reliability.

 Figure 6. A safe patch and update cycle.

Patching
A patch is an update that corrects software problems, including security weaknesses.

Supported Software
Using supported software matters because unsupported products may stop receiving security fixes.

Secure Configuration
Examples include screen locks, automatic updates, approved applications and disabling unnecessary sharing
features when policy requires it.

Change Carefully
Back up important work and follow school/work procedures before significant system changes.

LESSON 7: SAFE INTERNET, BROWSER AND NETWORK USE
Safe browsing and network habits reduce exposure to scams, unwanted downloads and information theft.

 Figure 7. Layered safe network and internet use.

Browser Safety
 Keep the browser updated.
 Check the site address carefully.
 Use HTTPS as one signal of encrypted transport, while remembering it does not prove a site is
 trustworthy.
 Avoid unexpected downloads.

Public Wi-Fi
Public networks may not be appropriate for sensitive activity. Follow organisational policy and prefer trusted
connections for important work.

Home or Office Network
Use approved router settings, strong administrative credentials and current firmware. Do not change network
equipment you are not authorised to manage.

Downloads
Obtain software from trusted official sources and verify that it is appropriate for the device.

Safe Guided Practical
1. Compare two fictional website addresses and identify which one appears suspicious.

2. Check that your browser is current.

3. List activities you would avoid on an untrusted public network.

LESSON 8: DATA PROTECTION, PRIVACY AND CLASSIFICATION
Information should be handled according to its sensitivity, purpose and applicable rules.

 Figure 8. A simple information-classification model.

Personal Information
Examples can include names, contact details, identification numbers and account information. Collect only
what is needed and protect it appropriately.

Classification
Organisations may label information Public, Internal, Confidential or another approved category.

Sharing
Before sharing, check the recipient, purpose, permissions and whether the chosen channel is appropriate.

Privacy
Privacy involves responsible collection, use, storage and sharing of information. Follow applicable policies
and laws.

LESSON 9: BACKUPS AND RECOVERY
Backups provide independent copies of important information so work can be recovered after loss or
disruption.

 Figure 9. The 3-2-1 backup concept.

3-2-1 Concept
A common resilience idea is three copies of important data, using two types of storage, with one copy kept
separately/offsite.

Backup Types
Organisations may use full, incremental or other backup methods depending on their needs.

Restore Testing
A backup should be tested because an unreadable or incomplete backup may not help during recovery.

Cloud Backup
Cloud services can support backup and synchronization, but access controls and account security still matter.

LESSON 10: ACCESS CONTROL AND LEAST PRIVILEGE
Access control determines who can use information or systems and what they are allowed to do.

 Figure 11. Role-based access and least privilege.

Authentication and Authorization
Authentication verifies identity. Authorization determines permissions after identity is established.

Least Privilege
Users should receive only the access needed for their responsibilities.

Role-Based Access
Permissions can be assigned to roles such as learner, instructor and administrator.

Account Lifecycle
Access should be reviewed when people join, change roles or leave an organisation.

LESSON 11: EMAIL AND MESSAGING SECURITY

 Figure 4. Phishing awareness also applies to business email.

Attachment Safety
Unexpected attachments should be treated cautiously. Verify the sender and context before opening.

Business Email Requests
Payment, password-reset or account-change requests deserve independent verification.

Sensitive Information
Avoid sending secrets or unnecessary confidential data through unsuitable channels.

Account Compromise Signs
Unexpected sent messages, login alerts or unexplained account changes should be reported promptly.

LESSON 12: MOBILE DEVICE AND CLOUD SECURITY
Phones, tablets and cloud services hold valuable information and require the same security discipline as
computers.

 Figure 7. Security layers apply to devices, networks, services and users.

Mobile Security
 Use a screen lock.
 Keep the operating system and apps updated.
 Install apps from trusted sources.
 Review app permissions.
 Enable approved device-location or remote-lock features when appropriate.

Cloud Security
 Use strong authentication.
 Review sharing permissions.
 Remove public links that are no longer needed.
 Protect recovery methods.
 Follow organisation policy for confidential data.

Lost Devices
Report loss promptly and follow approved remote-lock, account-protection and recovery procedures.

LESSON 14: INCIDENT RECOGNITION, REPORTING AND RESPONSE
A security incident is an event that may threaten confidentiality, integrity or availability and requires an
organised response.

 Figure 10. A safe beginner incident-response workflow.

Possible Warning Signs
 Unexpected account changes
 Unusual login alerts
 Missing or inaccessible files
 Suspicious messages sent from an account
 Lost device
 Unexpected security warnings

Immediate Actions
Follow the organisation's approved procedure. Limit further exposure when safe, report promptly and avoid
destroying useful evidence.

Escalation
Learners and ordinary users should contact the responsible administrator, teacher, parent/guardian or support
person rather than attempting risky technical intervention.

Recovery
Recovery can include password resets through official channels, restoring backups, updating systems and
reviewing what happened.

LESSON 15: CYBERSECURITY POLICIES, ETHICS AND LAW
Cybersecurity professionals must work within authorization, policy, ethics and applicable law.

 Figure 2. Security risk management begins with clear assets, responsibilities and controls.

Authorization
Only access systems, accounts and information you are explicitly authorised to use.

Responsible Practice
Do not test security controls on real people or systems without proper written permission and an approved
scope.

Policies
Organisations use acceptable-use, password, data-handling, backup, incident-response and access-control
policies.

Professional Ethics
Protect confidentiality, avoid conflicts of interest, document work accurately and escalate concerns
responsibly.

Legal Awareness
Privacy, computer-misuse and data-protection requirements vary by jurisdiction. Professionals must follow
applicable law and organisational guidance.

LESSON 16: RISK ASSESSMENT AND BUSINESS CONTINUITY
Organisations prepare for disruption by identifying important services, evaluating risks and planning how to
continue or recover operations.

 Figure 2. Risk assessment connects assets, threats, weaknesses and controls.

Risk Register
A risk register records assets, threats, weaknesses, existing controls, likelihood, impact and planned actions.

Business Impact
Some systems are more critical than others. Prioritise services that are essential to safety, learning, payments
or operations.

Continuity
Business continuity focuses on maintaining essential functions during disruption.

Disaster Recovery
Disaster recovery focuses on restoring technology and data after a serious event.

Review
Plans should be tested and updated as systems and responsibilities change.
